Borderland Lords and their Centrally-Held Sons: The Sasanian Empire’s Treatment of Captives after the Ad 450–1 Armenian Rebellion

ALFORD - Borderland Lords (JLAIBS 2026).pdf

p001 · p. 1 · abstract · 2 mentions

Abstract. This study examines the trajectory of two captive groups taken after the defeat of the AD 450–1 Armenian rebellion against Sasanian King Yazdgerd II and argues that the destinations and treatment of these captives must be understood in terms of their social positions, as well as when they were taken captive and the part they played in the rebellion. Sasanian authorities carefully delineated between the treatment of children, secular nobility, and Christian clerics involved in the rebellion, aiming both to regain control of the rebellious region and prosecute other defensive objectives along the empire’s northern border.

p003 · p. 1 · 5 mentions

On 2nd June 451 AD an Armenian rebellion was crushed by Sasanian forces at Awarayr near the region’s south-east border, decisively ending an uprising primarily comprised of Christian nobles that had begun the year prior in response to attempts by Yazdgerd II (r. AD 438–57) to convert the Armenian nobility to Zoroastrianism. The battle resulted in the death of rebel leader Vardan Mamikonean and many of his allies, who were canonised by the Armenian Church and became exemplars for resistance against foreign oppression. However, the following paper concerns itself not with these saints, but with survivors imprisoned after the rebellion.

p004 · p. 2 · 16 mentions

The fifth-century Armenian historian Łazar P‘arpec‘i includes two groups of captives in his History of the Armenians, one composed primarily of nobles who were initially sent to Gorgān (Latin. Hyrcania, the region north of the Alborz watershed and bordering the Caspian Sea to the south-east) and the other of noble children held in Khuzestan, with both groups also containing Christian priests. Łazar is a particularly valuable source for these captives, in part because he knew several of them personally. Most notably, his history was sponsored by Vahan Mamikonean – the eldest of three brothers who are some of the only identifiable captives of Khuzestan and who led his own rebellion against the Sasanians from AD 482–4. Łazar had been Vahan’s childhood playmate after the noble’s release, and was appointed as abbot of the monastery of Vałaršapat (modern Eǰmiacin) by Vahan c. 493 AD, writing to his patron to defend his conduct after the monks had him removed.

p005 · p. 2 · 11 mentions

The other primary historian for the AD 450–1 rebellion, Ełišē, only records the captives sent to Gorgān, but the differences between his and Łazar’s narratives make for useful comparison. Ełišē’s dating is a matter of significant debate. While traditionally considered an eye-witness to the rebellion, I am inclined towards Robert Thomson’s conclusion (following Akinean and Kiwlēsērean) that Ełišē wrote in the sixth century at the earliest. Such a dating better explains his reliance on Armenian translations of Philo and his parallels to John of Ephesus’ Ecclesiastical Histories by placing his work in the context of an Armenian community that was engaged in translating Greek philosophical and theological texts, and a Persian court culture that increasingly promoted interfaith debates.

p013 · p. 6 · ADULT AND CHILD CAPTIVES · 21 mentions

One Gorgān captive appearing only in Ełišē’s list, Nerseh Kamsarakan, may be identified as the future lord Nerseh of Širak, son of fellow captive Aršawir Kamsarakan and identifiably underage during his captivity. However, this Nerseh’s absence from Łazar’s list is extremely unusual, since Łazar had a close relationship with Nerseh. Łazar lived with Nerseh and his brother Hrahat from c. 470–82 AD, they continued to support him while he was abbot of Vałaršapat, and he cited Nerseh as a source for the c. 482 AD Battle of Akesga. Łazar elsewhere refers to a foster-brother of Nerseh from Aršamunik‘, indicating the lord had spent at least some of his childhood in southwestern Armenia. It is therefore unlikely that Nerseh spent fourteen years as a Persian captive.

p018 · p. 8 · MILITARY SERVICE · 11 mentions

The reason for this fortification and the probable conflict in which Armenian troops were used was the ongoing contest between the Sasanians and the Kidarite Huns. Competition between these two powers on the Sasanian eastern border intensified after Yazdgerd and Pērōz abandoned their predecessors’ strategy of paying off the Kidarites, ultimately resulting in the Kidarites’ destruction and replacement by the more powerful Hephthalite Huns. Yazdgerd paired increasing Sasanian defensive capacity with Zoroastrianising policies and numismatic reforms promoting Sasanian connections to the mythical Kayanian Zoroastrian dynasty whose foes had hailed from the same region as the Kidarites. In the long-term these transformed both the Sasanian ideology of power and defensive profile – solidifying borders and increasing imperial pressure on borderland elites – but in the short-term it probably also resulted in more-or-less constant conflict in the region of Tokharistan west of the Hindu Kush from AD 440–57.

p024 · p. 12 · KHUZESTAN · 12 mentions

The captives of Khuzestan comprised Vahan Mamikonean, his younger brothers Vasak and Artašēs, an unspecified number of children from other clans (including the related Kamsarakan family), and Bishop T‘at‘ik of Basean. Łazar claims they were captured by Vasak Siwni in AD 450 and kept in Siwnik‘ before being sent to the Persian court at some point before the other captives. He depicts the children being taken from their wet-nurses (dayeakk‘), although this does not necessarily signify they were infants, as dayeak could refer to more long-term childrearing or educational arrangements that sometimes continued until adulthood. Still, Łazar claims he was older than Vahan and that the children were still being educated by their mothers in AD 461 which, if accurate, would suggest all three were very young when captured.

p025 · p. 12 · KHUZESTAN · 6 mentions

Of the children, only the fates of the Mamikoneans are known. In AD 454/5, the trio were ransomed by their mother’s brother-in-law, a Georgian nobleman named Ašuša of Gugark‘, who took them to his home, where they were soon joined by their mother and a young Łazar P‘arpec‘i. The boys’ were probably released because they no longer had paternal relatives over whom their captivity could be leveraged, as their father and most senior uncle had been killed during the rebellion, while their surviving paternal uncle was imprisoned with three of their kinsmen. Ašuša himself had been a captive at the Sasanian court prior to the rebellion, and it is unclear whether he was released prior to ransoming his nephews or had remained in Persia the entire time.

p032 · p. 15 · PRIESTLY CAPTIVES · 6 mentions

The sole bishop executed in Asorestan – T‘at‘ik of Basean – is among the three priests who had not clearly breached Sasanian authority. While treated as one of the Łewondeank‘ by our sources, his death may have had more to do with the executions levied against his fellow Khuzestan captives. This would both imply that the risk to the Mamikonean boys was genuine, and that T‘at‘ik’s death was about removing unnecessary captives rather than taking vengeance on destructive clerics.

Seven Hellenes and One Christian in the Endless Peace Treaty of 532

SLA0104_03_Nechaeva_SevenHellenesandOneChristian.pdf

p004 · p. 3 · 1. AGATHIAS 2.30—31 · 19 mentions

According to Agathias, the seven Hellenic philosophers—Damascius of Syria, Simplicius of Cilicia, Eulamius of Phrygia, Priscian of Lydia, Hermes and Diogenes of Phoenicia, and Isidore of Gaza—moved to Persia “because they did not share the view of God prevailing among the Romans and thought that the Persian state was far better.” Attracted by the idealized picture of the land of “Plato’s philosopher king” and also because “they had been forbidden by law to live here in security since they did not subscribe to the existing order,” the seven are said to have moved to Persia with an intention to stay there permanently. We do not know any details about how they traveled. Agathias tells us, however, that although well received, the philosophers soon became disillusioned: the Persian way of life, religious practices, customs, and the king himself proved to be sources of disappointment. The historian concludes that for all these reasons the philosophers returned home as soon as possible.

p013 · p. 8 · Captive exchanges · 15 mentions

Sources also mention two individual exchanges of prominent captives that occurred as a result of this peace. A Roman military commander, Domitziolus (or Domnentiolus), a nephew of military commander Buzes, was taken prisoner by the Persians in the battle near Callinicum in April of 531. He was exchanged for a Persian nobleman, Izdegerd, a nephew of Hormizd, the ruler of Arzanene in Persarmenia, who had been taken prisoner by the Romans in the summer of 531. Pseudo-Zacharias’ description of the circumstances of the capture of Domitziolus is very detailed and probably follows some local Amidan source. The capture of Izdegerd is also referred to comprehensively and Pseudo-Zacharias is the only source that mentions Izdegerd and this exchange.

p014 · p. 9 · Captive exchanges · 11 mentions

Another exchange of captives is described by Procopius. He informs us that Dagaris, a bodyguard of one of the Roman military commanders (Sittas or Dorotheus), had previously been sent on a spy mission in Armenia and was captured by Hun allies of Persia in 530. He was then was returned to the Romans as a result of the treaty. In exchange, the Persians received “another man of no mean station.” Procopius characterizes the returned Dagaris as “an exceptionally able warrior” who later several times defeated the invading Huns. The historian, however, never mentions the name of the “prominent” Persian exchanged for him.

p018 · p. 11 · 3. THE MARTYRDOM OF MAR GRIGOR · 11 mentions

Around the year 518 or 521 Pīrān-Gušnasp converted to Christianity, taking the name Grigor (Mart. Grig. 5–6). The conversion led to persecution: Grigor’s property was confiscated and he was imprisoned for three years (Mart. Grig. 7). After three years of imprisonment, Grigor was pardoned and restored to his previous position, which he held for a long time afterwards (Mart. Grig. 8). During a Roman incursion into the region under his charge, Grigor was taken prisoner and deported, along with numerous other captives (Mart. Grig. 8). The general was brought to the Emperor (most likely already Justinian), who, impressed by the prominence of Grigor’s position in Persia, and especially by his faith, welcomed the captive with numerous gifts and an even more important position than Grigor had held in Persia (Mart. Grig. 8). Nothing is known from other sources about the allegedly high military appointment of Grigor in the Roman Empire. It was, however, not unusual for both states to employ renegades, defectors, and captives from the adversary’s state to utilize their expertise, especially in military affairs.

p019 · p. 12 · 3. THE MARTYRDOM OF MAR GRIGOR · 13 mentions

The Martyrium further informs us that in the third year of Chosroes’ reign, a Persian ambassador was sent to the Roman Emperor to conclude a peace treaty (“so that the peace spreads over the two kingdoms”), and that this envoy, Zabergān, asked the Emperor to extradite Mar Grigor. The Emperor was reluctant: first, because of the general’s military merits and, second, due to concern that Grigor would renounce his faith in Persia, where a person of his stature would not be allowed to remain Christian. According to the Martyrdom, however, Grigor himself asked to be released: as a convert of the Church of the East he was unhappy with the Chalcedonian environment in Constantinople. A remarkable detail is also reported by the Martyrdom: the Emperor made the Persian ambassador promise that Grigor would not be persecuted as a Christian “once he returns to the country of his fathers.” Zabergān agreed and ensured that everything necessary was done.
